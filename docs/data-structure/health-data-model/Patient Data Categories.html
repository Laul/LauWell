Patient Data Categories

Five categories a patient can hold in their head, each with a one-sentence test that settles where a new data point goes. Written to be the navigation layer of the app and the starting point for the entity model underneath it.

The dividing axis

Is this value read against a clinical normal range, or against a goal you set yourself? A blood pressure of 160/100 is abnormal to any clinician; nobody reads 4,000 steps as pathological. Clinical band → Vitals. Personal goal → Lifestyle.

This replaces the earlier mix of a shape-based rule for Vitals and a topic-based rule for Lifestyle. One question now decides both, and it rules correctly on the cases that broke the alternatives — mood and the menstrual cycle are daily logs without a clinical band, so they sit in Lifestyle without needing an exception.

The five, in detail

Example lists are illustrative. The authoritative row set is the data map further down.

Vitals clinical measurement

A measured value with a unit and a timestamp, read against a clinical normal range.

The strongest category in the set, because it describes a shape plus one interpretive test rather than a topic. Anything shaped like value + unit + timestamp, whose number a clinician would call normal or abnormal, is a Vital — so a sensor you connect in 2028 lands here with no new category and no new screen.

Lab results
By the rule these are Vitals — value, unit, timestamp, reference range — and the only difference from a home reading is who took the sample. One measurement entity with a source attribute, not a separate category. "Labs" can still be a visible section; it's a view over Vitals filtered by source.
Stoma output volume sits here, not in Lifestyle
Volume has a genuine clinical threshold — sustained high output risks dehydration — so the axis puts it in Vitals even though the rest of the ostomy routine is in Lifestyle. The volume is a measurement, the change routine is a log.

Medical Records standing fact + care record

A fact with a start date rather than a timestamp, or the record of an encounter with the health system.

Renamed from "Medical history", which was working against you — it signalled the past, so a patient wouldn't look there for their current diagnosis, and the active diagnosis list is one of the two things anyone needs in a consultation. Appointments, visits and providers now live here rather than in a sixth category: a visit is a record of something that happened, with a date, a person and usually a document, which is the same shape as everything else here.

A future appointment is not a record
Everything else here describes something settled; a scheduled visit describes something pending. Filing it here is fine, but upcoming appointments must also surface on Home — nobody opens an archive to find out what they're doing on Thursday.
Status, not deletion
A resolved condition stays visible as resolved rather than disappearing. The fact that something ended is itself clinical information.

Lifestyle daily log

Something I logged about my day, measured against a goal I set rather than a clinical range.

This is where the modifiable variables live — the levers you can actually pull — which makes it the input side of nearly every correlation the app will run. It also holds the ostomy routine: changes, wear time, emptying, accessories and leaks are daily facts about how the day went, which is what this category is for.

Sub-categories

Mood and cycle fit without an exception
Under an agency-based rule both would have been forced into Vitals, because you choose neither. Under the reference-range rule they're plainly Lifestyle: daily logs, no clinical band. That's the main reason this axis won.
Ostomy routine here, ostomy product in Treatments
The change event is a log entry; the appliance is a catalogue item with attributes. The product must stay a separate entity — that separation, not the filing, is what decides whether you can ever compare configurations.

Symptoms self-report

Something I feel and rate myself — no instrument involved.

The separation from Vitals is who produced the value: a device or a lab, versus you. That settles the awkward cases — a pain score is a number with a timestamp exactly like a blood pressure, but you generated it, so it's a Symptom. Sub-categories here follow body systems, which is what patients and clinicians both expect, with one general bucket for the records that aren't tied to a system.

The taxonomy problem
Clinical symptom vocabularies (SNOMED CT, ICD-11) are complete, rigorously classified, and hostile to browse. The answer is two vocabularies: a coded reference terminology underneath for correctness and any export to a clinician, and a patient-facing layer on top — everyday names, synonyms so "tummy ache" finds the right code, a body map, and a personal shortlist of the eight or ten symptoms you actually track. The sub-categories here are that patient-facing layer; they are not the terminology.
Two shapes live here
A symptom logged now is a measurement; a flare is an interval with a start, an end and a severity curve. Both read as Symptoms to the patient but they're different records.

Treatments & appliances intervention + catalogue

What I'm taking or using, and the product that delivers it.

With the ostomy routine in Lifestyle, this category settles into a cleaner job: it holds the things — drugs, appliances, accessories, devices — and the intervention records that govern them, while the day-to-day acts of using them are logged as Lifestyle.

Sub-categories
MedicationTherapies OstomyDevices SuppliesExperiments
Product and act must stay separate entities
The single most consequential decision in the set. Product attributes — convexity, flange size, barrier chemistry, brand, reference — belong to a catalogue entity; the Lifestyle change event references it. Only then can you ask "did the 25mm convex barrier give me longer wear time and better skin than the flat one". If product is a free-text field on the event, that question is permanently out of reach.
Experiments is a sub-category, not an afterthought
Trial periods are what make comparison possible. Mark labelled intervals — "on Coloplast X, convex, 30mm from 12 March" — rather than comparing all-time averages, which get confounded by season, diet and stoma maturation.

Where the model stands

Two tensions resolved in this revision, two still open.

01

Vitals and Lifestyle now share one axis

Previously Vitals was defined by shape and Lifestyle by topic, so sleep, steps and hydration qualified as both. The reference-range test settles it with one question and handles mood and the cycle without exceptions.

Resolved
02

"Records" stops hiding the active data, and absorbs encounters

The rename removes the signal that this is where old things live, and folding appointments, visits and providers in avoids a sixth category for what is structurally the same shape. Five holds.

Resolved
03

One category, two entities — and the model must say so

Treatments holds a catalogue and a log; Symptoms holds a symptom type and its occurrences; Medication holds the drug and the dose. In every case a reference table says what kinds of things exist and a log records what happened.

The shape column makes this visible: wherever Catalogue and Event share a sub-category, that's a type table plus an occurrence table. It's the same pattern four times over, which is the extensibility the navigation was aiming for.

Core modelling decision
04

Nothing in the five is an output

All five describe data going in. Trends, correlations, adherence rates and the appointment export are a layer over the five rather than a sixth sibling — correct, but it means the categories alone don't say what the app is for.

Only two rows in the whole map carry the Derived shape today. That's a fair reflection of a schema, and a reminder that the value sits in joins — Treatments against Symptoms, Lifestyle against Vitals — which need to be first-class rather than inferred at query time.

Design the joins deliberately

Still outside the five

Four things that deliberately aren't categories, and what they are instead.

01

Documents — an attachment, not a category

Lab PDFs, imaging, letters, product specs, stoma photos. They attach to records in all five categories, which is why Document appears as a shape rather than a home. Make it an attachment entity that hangs off any record, plus one "all files" view, and allow unattached files too.

02

Emergency view — derived, not stored

Blood type, active conditions, current medications, allergies, contacts. Every element already lives in Records and Treatments; what's missing is the assembly. It must be reachable without unlocking the app — any design where finding a blood type takes two taps has failed at the only moment it mattered.

03

Provenance — fields on every record

Which device, which lab, entered by hand or synced, edited since. When two sources disagree about the same measurement you need a rule, and when you show a clinician a number they will ask where it came from.

04

Free text — attaches anywhere

The note that doesn't fit a field: what the nurse said, why today was different, a question to remember. A note should attach to any record and also stand alone with a date. Every structured system needs its escape hatch, or people stop using the structured parts.

Where this leaves things

Five categories, thirty-five sub-categories, seven storage shapes, and a named home for every data point the app is expected to hold. Nothing in the set requires a patient to learn a vocabulary, and nothing in the shape column requires a developer to guess at a table.

The thing to protect from here is the Catalogue plus Event pattern. It looks like an implementation detail and it is actually the feature — the difference between an app that records that you changed a pouch and one that can tell you which pouch works better for you.

Underneath, Vitals, Lifestyle and the measured part of Symptoms are all the same storage shape. Category and sub-category are facets on the data type, not separate tables, so if an assignment turns out wrong in six months it's a config row rather than a migration.
